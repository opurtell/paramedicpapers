Intranasal dexmedetomidine paediatric sedation

Intranasal Dexmedetomidine for Paediatric Procedural Sedation in the Emergency Department: A Single‐Centre Retrospective Observational Cohort Study
Emergency Medicine Australasia · Added 22 September 2026

Townsville cohort of 114 children given intranasal dexmedetomidine for ED procedural sedation — effective as a sole agent in 78%, adverse events in 13% (mostly mild bradycardia), but ED stays ran long at a median 395 minutes

Study type
Cohort
Design
Single-centre retrospective observational cohort after guideline implementation
Participants
114 children aged 1–16 years in Townsville ED
Main finding
IN dexmedetomidine successful as sole agent in 78.1% (95% CI 69.6–84.7%); adverse events 13.2%, mostly mild bradycardia; median ED length of stay 395 min; younger age associated with higher success (aOR 0.86 per year)
Caveat
Single-centre retrospective design with incomplete documentation of sedation depth and satisfaction
Bottom line
Hypothesis-generating

Why it matters in Australia (Low relevance). transferability — intranasal dexmedetomidine isn't in the ACTAS formulary and procedural sedation stays in ED hands — interesting Australian evidence should a scope debate ever arrive

Learning goal

Weigh intranasal dexmedetomidine against other sedation options for paediatric procedural sedation in my practice.

  • Paediatric IV ketamine sedation very safe

    Annals of Emergency Medicine · 19 Sep 2026 · Systematic review · Supports current practice

    Systematic review of 20 studies covering 67,871 children having IV ketamine procedural sedation in EDs — no deaths or permanent adverse outcomes, sentinel events pooled at about 1 in 11,558 sedations, serious adverse events 0.34%, with age ≥10, upper respiratory infection and opioid co-administration modest predictors while ASA status and benzodiazepine co-administration were not.

  • Anonymous video deletion biased newborn RCT

    Resuscitation · 18 Sep 2026 · Other · Context only

    Norwegian analysis of the NeoBeat RCT testing whether anonymous staff deletion of resuscitation videos introduced selection bias — after the anonymous opt-out, enrolled newborns had significantly higher Apgar scores, shorter ventilation and fewer admissions, and the trial was terminated halfway without power to conclude on its outcomes.
