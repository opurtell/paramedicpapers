Real-time feedback tidies BVM ventilation in arrest

Real-time feedback during bag-valve-mask ventilation for out-of-hospital cardiac arrest: a before-and-after study (VEntilation during Cardiac Arrest Resuscitation Study, VECARS-2)
Resuscitation · 2026-10 · Added 22 October 2026

A Paris before-and-after study of 163 arrests found real-time feedback during bag-valve-mask ventilation improved insufflated-volume adherence (68% vs 42% of breaths in the 400–600 mL range) and cut leak volume by 12%, although expired tidal volumes were unchanged.

Study type
Cohort
Design
Before-and-after observational study
Participants
4,300 ventilations in 163 adults with OHCA
Main finding
With feedback, insufflated volume fell within guideline range in 68% of ventilations vs 42%, dispersion dropped 35%, and leak volume was 12% lower (mean ratio 0.88); expired tidal volume was unchanged
Caveat
Before-and-after design without randomisation, single basic-life-support system, and no patient outcomes reported
Bottom line
Hypothesis-generating

Why it matters in Australia (High relevance). Bag-valve-mask ventilation during 30:2 CPR is standard practice on every Australian ambulance, so ventilation-quality feedback speaks directly to how crews are taught and audited.

Learning goal

Strengthen my ability to deliver consistent tidal volumes during bag-valve-mask ventilation in cardiac arrest.

  • Transport ventilation during CPR feasible in pilot

    BMC Emergency Medicine · 28 Oct 2026 · Other · Hypothesis-generating

    A Thai exploratory pilot compared transport mechanical ventilation (HAMILTON-T1, volume-controlled) with bag-valve ventilation during CPR in adult OHCA and found no significant differences in PCO2, pH, pO2, sustained ROSC or 28-day survival, with mechanical ventilation operationally feasible.

  • Airway strategy and survival in Polish HEMS

    Scandinavian Journal of Trauma, Resuscitation and Emergency Medicine · 18 Oct 2026 · Registry · Hypothesis-generating

    A retrospective analysis of Poland's national physician-staffed HEMS registry tested airway strategies in 8,980 intubated adults against survival to hospital handover — HEMS-performed intubation was most common (49.8%), and in non-traumatic cardiac arrest a sequential supraglottic-airway-then-intubation strategy showed no significant difference from HEMS-performed intubation, while some other strategies carried higher unadjusted odds of not being alive at handover.
