Hot days drive dizziness call-outs

Association between meteorological conditions and dizziness-related ambulance transports: a population-based study in Kochi Prefecture, Japan
Environmental Health and Preventive Medicine · 2026 · Added 24 October 2026

Population-based study of 270,120 emergency transports in Kochi, Japan linking weather to dizziness-related ambulance call-outs — hotter days and short-term temperature spikes raised the odds of high-volume dizziness days, peaking in summer.

Study type
Cohort
Design
Retrospective population-based cohort (2017–2023)
Participants
270,120 emergency transports, 8,981 dizziness-related
Main finding
Higher mean air temperature consistently associated with increased odds of high-frequency dizziness-transport days (≥5/day), as were short-term maximum-temperature increases and smaller diurnal temperature ranges; most frequent in summer
Caveat
Modest absolute differences in meteorological variables and dizziness defined by receiving-facility physician diagnosis
Bottom line
Context only

Why it matters in Australia (Medium relevance). A Japanese physician-diagnosed dataset, but heat-driven demand spikes are a planning problem Australian ambulance services face every summer.

Learning goal

Understand how heat and temperature swings influence demand patterns and my management of dizziness presentations in Australian summer conditions.
