Papers in this episode

  • Rescue breaths linked to better paediatric arrest survival

    JAMA Network Open · 21 Nov 2026 · Registry · Hypothesis-generating

    A 19-year Japanese national registry cohort of 26,785 children with OHCA found compression-only bystander CPR rose from 14.6% to 55.1% of cases as rescue-breathing CPR fell from 41.7% to 10.4%, and rescue-breathing CPR was associated with higher 30-day neurologically favourable survival than compression-only CPR (9.5%; 3.6% with no bystander CPR).

  • Incomplete on-scene care in paediatric cardiac arrest

    Prehospital Emergency Care · 21 Nov 2026 · Registry · Hypothesis-generating

    A US retrospective analysis of 176 paediatric non-traumatic OHCA cases in Los Angeles County found goal-directed on-scene interventions before transport were often incomplete — adrenaline in only 41% of non-shockable arrests — with 6% survival to discharge.

  • Blood stored on ambulances stays within limits to day 42

    Prehospital Emergency Care · 21 Nov 2026 · RCT · Hypothesis-generating

    A French randomised experimental study stored 12 red cell units continuously in operational ground ambulances for 35 days and found all units stayed within the European 0.8% haemolysis limit at day 42, though ambulance-stored units showed more haemolysis and storage-induced microerythrocytes than blood-bank controls.

  • Blood biomarker assay fits inside an EMS transport window

    Trauma Surgery & Acute Care Open · 21 Nov 2026 · Simulation · Hypothesis-generating

    A US pilot simulation study had 24 paramedics run a whole-blood point-of-care GFAP/UCH-L1 bioassay workflow (i-STAT Alinity with teleconsent via Pulsara) for simulated mild TBI and found a median 6.5 minutes from consent to assay completion with over 90% rating the training sufficient.

Transcript

Twelve new papers today — here are the top four. Paper one — bystanders who gave rescue breaths during paediatric cardiac arrest had better survival with good brain function than those doing compression-only CPR. Paper two — US crews often left the scene of a paediatric arrest without giving adrenaline. Paper three — red blood cells stored on ambulances stayed within safety limits for six weeks. Paper four — a point-of-care blood test for head injury fit inside a normal transport window.

Paper one — JAMA Network Open. A nationwide registry cohort from Japan, just under twenty-seven thousand children with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest between two thousand five and twenty twenty-three. The question is which type of bystander CPR gives children the best chance. Compression-only CPR has been heavily promoted to get laypeople acting, and the data shows the message landed — compression-only rose from about fifteen per cent of cases to fifty-five, while rescue-breathing CPR fell from forty-two per cent to ten. But survival cut against that shift. Thirty-day survival with favourable brain function was nine and a half per cent with compression-only CPR, and three point six per cent with no bystander CPR at all — with rescue-breathing CPR the best of the three. It's registry data, so sicker children may simply be getting different types of CPR — this can't prove cause. But Australian resuscitation guidance has always kept rescue breaths in the paediatric chain, and this suggests that's worth defending against compression-only messaging drifting across from adult arrest.

Paper two — Prehospital Emergency Care. A retrospective registry review from Los Angeles County — a hundred and seventy-six children under fifteen with non-traumatic cardiac arrest, median age just ten months. Nine in ten had a non-shockable rhythm, and among those, crews gave adrenaline before transport in only forty-one per cent of cases, and fluids in just eight per cent. Defibrillation looked better — eighty-five per cent of the few shockable arrests got a shock on scene. Median scene time was ten minutes. Six per cent survived to discharge, three per cent with favourable brain function. The uncomfortable question is whether an early-transport habit is crowding out on-scene care in paediatric arrests. It's one US region and a small sample, so treat it as hypothesis-generating. But adrenaline is standard kit across Australian services, so the on-scene-versus-transport question lands here too.

Paper three — Prehospital Emergency Care again. A randomised experimental study run under a French physician-staffed service: twelve units of red blood cells stored continuously in working ambulances from day seven to day forty-two, against fourteen kept in a standard blood bank fridge. Every ambulance-stored unit stayed under the European haemolysis limit of zero point eight per cent at day forty-two. The caveats: ambulance units did show measurably more haemolysis and more storage-damaged cells, mostly in the final fortnight, the sample was too small to rule out bigger differences, and no patients were actually transfused. Prehospital blood is still limited to a handful of Australian services, but for the ones running blood on trucks, this is reassuring on shelf life.

Paper four — Trauma Surgery and Acute Care Open. A pilot simulation study — twenty-four paramedics in Maryland ran a whole-blood point-of-care assay for mild head injury, measuring brain biomarkers that flag intracranial bleeding on a handheld device, with consent taken remotely over a mobile platform. Median six and a half minutes from consent to result — comfortably inside a typical transport interval. Over ninety per cent rated the training sufficient, and every participant said they'd adopt it. The big caveat: simulated saline instead of a real blood draw, so no diagnostic accuracy was tested. Devices like this aren't something Australian ambulances carry — adoption would take funded kit and a guideline change — but the workflow demonstrably fits the job.

Quick recap. One — Japanese registry data links bystander rescue breaths to better outcomes in paediatric arrest. Two — US crews often reached hospital after a paediatric arrest without giving adrenaline on scene. Three — blood stored on ambulances stayed within safety limits to day forty-two. Four — a point-of-care head-injury blood test ran in under seven minutes in simulation.
