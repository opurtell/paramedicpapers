Papers in this episode

  • Prehospital TXA lowers mortality in bleeding trauma

    BMC Emergency Medicine · 19 Nov 2026 · Systematic review · Supports current practice

    A systematic review of randomised evidence, authored in Saudi Arabia, pooled three trials of prehospital TXA in trauma and found lower 28–30-day mortality (RR 0.81, 95% CI 0.68–0.95) without a significant rise in thromboembolic events or seizures.

  • Bystander-attached AEDs linked with best OHCA survival

    Resuscitation · 19 Nov 2026 · Registry · Hypothesis-generating

    A Swedish registry cohort of 9,413 OHCAs in Västra Götaland found bystander-attached AEDs were associated with the highest 30-day survival (28% vs 9.0% when EMS attached first, OR 2.25), pointing to earlier defibrillation as the key lever.

  • First responder alerts linked to more defibrillation

    Resuscitation · 19 Nov 2026 · Cohort · Hypothesis-generating

    A Danish registry study of 5,753 OHCAs found HeartRunner community-first-responder activation was associated with more bystander defibrillation at every AED distance (TMLE +6.1 pp) and a modest 1.8-point gain in 30-day survival.

  • Mobile app lifts simulated trauma performance

    Prehospital Emergency Care · 19 Nov 2026 · RCT · Hypothesis-generating

    A US crossover RCT found paramedic pairs using a mobile app with protocols, decision-support tools and procedure videos completed all critical actions in simulated trauma far more often than with standard practice (35.4% vs 6.3%).

Transcript

Nineteen new papers today — here are the top four. Paper one — pooled randomised-trial data backs prehospital TXA in bleeding trauma, with lower month mortality and no clear safety signal. Paper two — Swedish registry data finds bystander-attached defibrillators beat crew-attached ones for survival. Paper three — a Danish first-responder alert app pushed more bystanders to defibrillate, and nudged survival up. Paper four — a decision-support app helped paramedic pairs complete simulated trauma far more reliably.

Paper one — BMC Emergency Medicine. A systematic review pooling only randomised trials of prehospital tranexamic acid — three of them: PATCH-Trauma, STAAMP and ROC-TXA. Across those trials, giving TXA before hospital was linked with lower death at twenty-eight to thirty days — a relative reduction of about nineteen percent. The brain-injury and in-hospital mortality numbers each came from a single trial and didn't reach significance on their own. On safety, no statistically significant jump in clots or seizures, though the clot estimates sit high enough to keep watching. Here in Australia, TXA is in the kits of nearly every ambulance service — the ACT is the notable exception — so this pooled evidence speaks directly to what most crews already do. The caveat: the whole mortality result rests on just three trials.

Paper two — Resuscitation. A Swedish registry cohort: nine thousand four hundred and thirteen out-of-hospital arrests in western Sweden over ten years. Ambulance crews attached the first defibrillator in seventy-two percent of cases; bystanders, only six percent. But survival ran the other way — thirty-day survival was twenty-eight percent when a bystander attached the first AED, against nine percent when the crew did. In witnessed shockable arrests it reached sixty-two percent with bystander attachment. And the association held after adjustment, with more than double the odds of survival. The likely driver is simply earlier defibrillation. The catch: it's observational — bystander attachment probably flags arrests that were recognised and treated sooner, so the credit can't go to the responder alone.

Paper three — also Resuscitation. Danish registry linkage this time: five thousand seven hundred and fifty-three arrests, testing HeartRunner, an app that alerts nearby community first responders when an arrest is called in. Alerts went out in forty-one percent of cases, and they blunted the effect of distance to the nearest public defibrillator — predicted bystander defibrillation ran about ten percent versus five and a half close to an AED, and thirteen versus under five when the nearest device was a kilometre or more away. Formal modelling put the alert effect at six extra defibrillations per hundred arrests, and roughly two extra survivors per hundred at thirty days. Small on survival, real on defibrillation. Not randomised, though, and the survival confidence interval just grazes zero.

Paper four — Prehospital Emergency Care. A crossover randomised controlled trial from the US: ninety-eight paramedics in forty-eight pairs, run through high-fidelity adult and paediatric trauma simulations with and without a mobile app carrying local protocols, decision-support tools and procedure videos. With the app, thirty-five percent of scenarios met every critical action. Without it, six percent. The paediatric gap was starkest — thirty percent completion with the app, none without. Pairs rated it practical and valuable. Big caveat: it's simulation at a single US agency, not patient outcomes — but it suggests in-app decision support is more than a gimmick, and the interesting question for Australian services is how that sits alongside the app-based guidelines crews already carry.

Quick recap. One — pooled randomised-trial data supports prehospital TXA, with about nineteen percent lower mortality and no clear clot signal. Two — bystander-attached defibrillators were linked with the best Swedish survival, more than double crew attachment. Three — a Danish responder-alert app lifted bystander defibrillation by six points and survival by two. Four — a protocol-and-decision-support app lifted trauma simulation completion from six percent to thirty-five.
