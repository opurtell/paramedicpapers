Transcript

Three papers today. Paper one — a centralised call centre in Turkey cut calls per incident nearly in half and ambulance response times by thirty-seven per cent. Paper two — seventy per cent of fatal brain bleeds in Finland and New Zealand kill before the patient ever reaches a neurosurgical unit. Paper three — an Australian breathlessness self-management service, and one in five of its patients says it stopped them calling an ambulance.

Paper one — Pakistan Journal of Medical Sciences. A retrospective before-and-after study from Ordu province in Turkey, covering two point three million emergency calls and nearly seven hundred and twenty-two thousand cases over seven years. Before the change, every incident generated about four and a half calls; after a centralised emergency call centre took over, that dropped to under two and a half — a forty-nine per cent fall. Ambulance response time fell thirty-seven per cent, mobilisation time nearly twenty-four per cent, and on-time arrival improved in both city and country areas — the rural rate went from eighty-two to eighty-nine per cent. The idea is lean management: a single call centre removes duplicate calls, wasted mobilisations and round-trips. The big caveat is the design — a simple before-and-after with no adjustment for anything else that changed over those seven years, so not all of that gain can be credited to the call centre. Still, it's a nice systems-level reminder that the biggest efficiency wins often happen before a crew even turns a wheel.

Paper two — JAMA Network Open. A nationwide cohort from Finland and New Zealand, nearly thirteen thousand patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage, tracked across every hospital district in both countries. Of the five and a half thousand deaths within thirty days, seventy per cent occurred without the patient ever being admitted to a neurosurgical unit — and the figure was essentially identical in both countries. It also climbed with distance: districts within a hundred and thirty kilometres of a neurosurgical centre ran about seventy per cent, and the further out you go, the worse it gets. What that says is that for most people who die of a ruptured aneurysm, definitive care was never realistically on the table — which puts the weight on early recognition and smart destination decisions in the field. It's observational administrative data, so distance is associated with death rather than proven to cause it, but the headline number is hard to argue with.

Paper three — BMC Pulmonary Medicine. A co-designed feasibility study from Australia of a home-based breathlessness intervention service — a programme that coaches people with chronic illness in non-drug strategies like positioning, breathing techniques and handheld fans. A hundred and forty patients completed it over twenty-two months, missing the original goal of a hundred within twelve, and reaching people who were older, less mobile, and couldn't access rehabilitation. Ninety per cent reported improvement in daily activities, seventy per cent in their worst breathlessness — and the one that jumps out for me: twenty-one per cent said they'd avoided calling an ambulance for breathlessness using the strategies they'd learned. It's a single-arm study with no control group and self-reported outcomes, so treat the numbers as encouraging rather than proven. But for crews dealing with frequent breathlessness callers, this points at something upstream that might actually reduce those callouts.

Quick recap. One — a Turkish call centre study: calls per incident down forty-nine per cent, response times down thirty-seven. Two — seventy per cent of fatal brain bleeds never reach a neurosurgical unit, and worse the further out you live. Three — an Australian breathlessness self-management service, with one in five patients avoiding an ambulance call.
