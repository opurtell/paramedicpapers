Transcript

Four papers today. Paper one — most prehospital tourniquets were applied without justification, yet caused no extra harm. Paper two — gastric distension after cardiac arrest didn't differ between tracheal tubes and supraglottic airways. Paper three — most crews who meet a cardiac arrest with a do-not-resuscitate order still start resuscitation and transport. Paper four — a systematic review asking whether smaller groups teach resuscitation better than large ones.

Paper one — Rhode Island Medical Journal. A retrospective review of two hundred and thirty-two patients who had prehospital tourniquets placed, drawn from a Level one trauma centre registry over a six-year period. Sixty-nine point four percent of those tourniquets were unjustified by the researchers' criteria. The justified cases were sicker — lower blood pressures, higher shock index — and more often needed surgery or transfusion. But the unjustified group showed no difference in mortality, compartment syndrome or nerve palsy. So a permissive approach to tourniquets looks safe in this cohort — though the authors flag the education gap rather than declare open season. Caveats: a single US centre, a retrospective chart review, and the justification criteria were researcher-defined. Bottom line — supports current practice, but it fuels the low-threshold debate rather than settling it. Worth noting the transfusion bit: prehospital blood is still limited to a few Australian services, so the "sicker and needing blood" signal doesn't land evenly here.

Paper two — Resuscitation. A German retrospective study used CT volumetry to measure gastric volumes after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest — one hundred and twenty patients who reached a whole-body CT within a hundred and eighty minutes of emergency department arrival, out of two hundred and fifty-five screened. Median total gastric volume was five hundred and ninety-four millilitres — high. Among patients arriving with an advanced airway in place, there was no significant difference in gastric volume between the tracheal tube group — eighty-seven patients — and the supraglottic group — twenty-eight. Aspiration on CT was numerically more common with supraglottic airways, seventy-eight point six versus fifty-seven point five percent, but that didn't reach significance — and gastric volume itself didn't track with aspiration at all. Caveats: retrospective, single-centre, and limited to patients well enough to reach an early CT. Hypothesis-generating, not practice-changing.

Paper three — BMC Emergency Medicine. A mixed-methods survey of three hundred and two Japanese ambulance personnel, with thematic analysis of a hundred and eleven free-text responses. Just over half had attended a cardiac arrest where a do-not-attempt-resuscitation directive existed — and among those, eighty percent still performed basic life support and ninety-one percent transported. Four themes explain why: inadequate decision-making and communication processes around the directives, no system rules that accommodate them, conflicts between what crews are permitted to do and what patients and families want, and the emotional burden of working an arrest that shouldn't be worked. The Japanese crews operate under tighter legal constraints than Australian paramedics do, but the pattern — absent or unclear directives forcing treatment on scene — is recognisable here in Australia. Context more than action.

Paper four — Resuscitation Plus. A systematic review comparing smaller versus larger group sizes in resuscitation training. The abstract isn't published yet, so there are no results to report. Worth watching — group size is a universal training question for Australian services.

Quick recap. One — nearly seventy percent of prehospital tourniquets were unjustified, yet none of the feared complications followed. Two — gastric distension after cardiac arrest was high but didn't differ between airway types, and didn't track with aspiration. Three — crews meeting a cardiac arrest with a valid directive still resuscitate and transport most of the time, driven by system-level gaps. Four — a group-size review in resuscitation training is out, results pending.
