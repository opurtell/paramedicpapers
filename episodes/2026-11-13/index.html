Papers in this episode

  • GCS-based triage misses seriously injured trauma patients

    BMC Emergency Medicine · 13 Nov 2026 · Cohort · Hypothesis-generating

    A South Korean linked EMS–hospital cohort of 5,841 suspected severe trauma patients found weak agreement between prehospital GCS classification and diagnosis-based injury severity (κ 0.192), with one in ten patients (10.0%) severely injured despite GCS 9–15 and destination undertriage of 33.6% (GCS-based) and 47.3% (diagnosis-based), roughly doubled in rural areas.

  • Rhythm changes during CPR track neurological outcomes

    Critical Care Medicine · 13 Nov 2026 · Registry · Hypothesis-generating

    A South Korean nationwide registry study of adults with initial nonshockable OHCA found rhythm transitions during CPR graded neurological outcome: asystole converting to PEA was linked to better outcomes than persistent asystole (OR 2.28), and persistent PEA carried the best odds (OR 8.70 versus persistent asystole).

  • Night-time OHCA outcomes linked to bystander availability

    Journal of Critical Care Medicine · 13 Nov 2026 · Systematic review · Hypothesis-generating

    A Czech-led scoping review of 18 studies found OHCA and STEMI incidence peaks in the morning with a secondary afternoon/evening peak, and night-time events were linked to lower ROSC and worse one-month and one-year survival, explained by limited bystander availability, less bystander CPR and defibrillation and longer times to hospital intervention rather than time of day itself.

  • Higher admission PaCO2 linked to worse post-arrest survival

    Journal of Critical Care Medicine · 13 Nov 2026 · Cohort · Hypothesis-generating

    A Chinese single-centre retrospective cohort of 617 adults resuscitated from OHCA found admission PaCO2 above 50 mmHg associated with higher one-month mortality in a stepwise gradient (each 10-mmHg increase aOR 1.45, 95% CI 1.24–1.70).

Transcript

Seven new papers today — here are the top four. Paper one — GCS-based trauma triage misses one in ten severe injuries. Paper two — rhythm changes during CPR track neurological outcome. Paper three — night-time cardiac arrests do worse because fewer bystanders step in. Paper four — high carbon dioxide on the post-arrest blood gas tracks with mortality.

Paper one — BMC Emergency Medicine. A linked ambulance-to-hospital cohort from South Korea, just under six thousand patients with suspected severe trauma. The question: does the prehospital Glasgow Coma Scale line up with how severely people are actually injured? Barely. A GCS of eight or less picked up just over half of the patients with severe injuries on final diagnosis, and one in ten patients with severe injuries on the diagnosis-based criterion had a GCS between nine and fifteen — a group GCS-based classification can't see. Destination undertriage ran at about a third under the GCS definition and nearly half under the diagnosis-based one, and it roughly doubled in rural areas — a pattern that maps closely onto Australian geography. Caveat: severity was measured after the fact from hospital diagnosis codes, so this is hypothesis-generating rather than proof.

Paper two — Critical Care Medicine. A nationwide registry of out-of-hospital cardiac arrests in South Korea, six years of adults who started in a nonshockable rhythm. The researchers grouped arrests by whether the rhythm stayed put or transitioned during CPR. The pattern graded neatly: persistent asystole had the worst neurological outcomes, asystole converting to PEA did a bit more than twice as well, and arrests that stayed in PEA had nearly nine times the odds of a good outcome versus persistent asystole. The absolute numbers are tiny — fractions of a percent — so this is prognosis, not practice change. But it suggests what a crew sees on the rhythm check every two minutes carries real information about where the arrest is heading.

Paper three — the Journal of Critical Care Medicine. A scoping review of eighteen studies on circadian patterns in cardiac arrest and STEMI. Incidence peaks in the morning, with a second bump in the afternoon and evening, and the quietest hours overnight. The striking part: night-time arrests had lower rates of return of spontaneous circulation and worse survival at one month and one year — but not because of the hour itself. It's what's around at that hour: fewer bystanders, less bystander CPR and defibrillation, and longer waits to hospital intervention. These events were also more frequent in winter. The actionable part isn't clinical — it supports community CPR and defibrillator awareness, which matters here in Australia as much as anywhere.

Paper four — also the Journal of Critical Care Medicine. A retrospective cohort of six hundred and seventeen adults resuscitated from cardiac arrest at a single centre, looking at the carbon dioxide level on the first blood gas after arrival. Mortality climbed stepwise: about fifty percent when the carbon dioxide reading was fifty or below, over eighty percent between fifty and seventy, and over ninety percent above seventy. Each ten-millimetre rise carried about forty-five percent higher odds of death at one month, with a matching gradient for neurological outcome. Strictly associational — a high reading may simply mark a sicker arrest. But that first blood gas partly reflects prehospital ventilation, which is squarely the part a crew controls, so the ventilation being run on the way in matters.

Quick recap. One — GCS-based triage misses one in ten severe injuries, and undertriage is worst in the country. Two — rhythm transitions during CPR grade neurological outcome. Three — night-time arrests do worse because bystanders are scarce, not because of the hour. Four — higher carbon dioxide on the post-arrest blood gas tracks with death, and ventilation is the crew-controlled piece of that.

Also on the dashboard today — NSW data linkage tracking sudden cardiac arrest, children's participation in ambulance care, and a review of newborn positive pressure ventilation at birth. That's the roundup for Friday the thirteenth of November. Back tomorrow.
