Eight new papers today — here are the top four. Paper one — reversed airflow was near-universal in intubated cardiac arrest patients on transport ventilators, with compressions forcing air back out of the lungs on most breaths. Paper two — coagulopathy was common in trauma patients who received prehospital blood on long transports, and it tracked with higher mortality. Paper three — electronic patient care records were nearly twice as complete as paper. Paper four — non-transport response vehicles matched full transport units on stroke code times.

Paper one — Resuscitation. A physiology study from a physician-staffed prehospital team, twelve intubated adults in cardiac arrest getting continuous compressions and volume-controlled ventilation from a transport ventilator. The question was how often compressions land during the inspiratory phase and push air back out — reversed airflow. The answer: ten of the twelve patients, and eighty-two per cent of the ventilations analysed, with a median of sixty-one millilitres forced back out per episode. The number that jumps out at me is how consistent it was — this isn't an occasional artefact, it's most breaths. Big caveat, though: twelve patients at one centre, no outcome data at all, so the clinical consequences are unknown. Intubation with ventilator care is an intensive-care paramedic skill in most Australian services, so crews at that level are the ones this speaks to. Hypothesis-generating, not practice-changing.

Paper two — BMC Emergency Medicine. A retrospective cohort from Canada, seventy trauma patients flown by air ambulance who received prehospital transfusion, with a median out-of-hospital time of one hundred and thirty-one minutes. On arrival at the trauma centre, sixteen per cent had an INR over one point five, and twenty per cent had fibrinogen below one point five. An INR over one point five was associated with increased mortality, and half the cohort had a massive haemorrhage protocol activated within the hour. So even with blood on board and early transfusion, coagulopathy was still showing up by the door. Prehospital blood is still limited to a handful of Australian services, and transport intervals like these are rare here — this one informs the debate more than current practice. It's also a small retrospective pilot, mostly univariable analyses.

Paper three — International Journal of Emergency Medicine. A German retrospective cohort of two thousand seven hundred and eighty-one prehospital physician reports for suspected acute coronary syndrome. Records kept electronically were ninety-one per cent complete against a predefined handover data set — paper records managed forty-eight per cent. After adjustment, electronic documentation carried roughly ten-fold higher odds of a complete record. The biggest gaps were in exactly the things that matter at handover: blood pressures and oxygen saturations handed over, prehospital times, and twelve-lead ECG documentation. Electronic records are now standard across Australian ambulances — the interesting question this raises is whether the completeness benefit is actually showing up in local handovers, which is worth an audit. One caveat: the data's from twenty fourteen to twenty eighteen, and documentation systems have moved since.

Paper four — Emergencias. A retrospective single-service cohort from Spain, one thousand two hundred and sixty-three stroke codes with known onset times. The comparison: advanced life support response vehicles that can't transport, backed up by a basic transport ambulance, versus full transport intensive care units. Call-to-assessment times were identical — twenty-seven minutes in both groups. Assessment-to-door was forty-four versus forty-six minutes, and there were no differences in stroke diagnosis at discharge or reperfusion treatment rates. The model — an advanced-care car with a transport crew behind it — is one several Australian services already run in some form, and this supports it not costing anything on the clock for stroke.

Quick recap. One — reversed airflow turned up in more than eight in ten ventilations during mechanical compressions. Two — one in five transfused trauma patients arrived with fibrinogen below one point five, and coagulopathy tracked with mortality. Three — electronic records were nearly twice as complete as paper, with the gaps right where handover needs them. Four — non-transport response vehicles matched transport units on stroke times and reperfusion.
