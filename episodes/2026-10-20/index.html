Transcript

Four papers to get through today. Paper one — the biggest study of PEA cardiac arrest yet, showing which prehospital signs predict a good outcome, and how the answer changes with the presumed cause. Paper two — a drone system that measures vital signs from the air and triages mass-casualty scenes automatically. Paper three — Australian data showing alcohol harms rising even as reported drinking falls. Paper four — interprofessional simulation changing how paramedic students see their own role.

Paper one — Resuscitation Plus. A nationwide registry cohort from Japan — four hundred and forty-two thousand, six hundred and thirty adults with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest and an initial rhythm of PEA, spanning twenty years. The question: which prehospital factors predict a favourable neurological outcome at one month? A witnessed arrest predicted a good outcome, and longer response times predicted worse ones. Prehospital return of spontaneous circulation was by far the strongest predictor by hospital arrival. The interesting twist: conversion to a shockable rhythm predicted a good outcome only when the PEA was cardiac in origin — and a worse one when it wasn't. And the apparent harm from adrenaline and advanced airways largely washed out once the analysis properly accounted for timing. The caveat: this is registry data, the cause of each arrest was presumed rather than confirmed, and none of it is causal. Still, it's a useful reminder that PEA isn't one beast — the same sign can point opposite ways depending on why the heart stopped.

Paper two — Prehospital and Disaster Medicine. A system development study with a pilot test, describing a drone-integrated artificial intelligence setup for mass-casualty triage. The drone captures video and thermal imagery, and algorithms measure heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and temperature without touching a single patient — video-based pulse tracking, thermal analysis around the nose, and neural networks. It then generates triage decision support and streams each casualty's location, vital signs and triage category to response teams and dispatch. It worked under controlled indoor conditions — and that's the limit. Real mass-casualty scenes bring smoke, crowds, weather and chaos, and accuracy there is untested. Nothing like this is in operational use in Australia, but it's a fair signal of where remote triage tech is heading.

Paper three — Drug and Alcohol Review. A synthesis of thirteen years of national Australian data, from twenty-ten to twenty-twenty-three, using joinpoint regression across consumption and harm measures. The headline: survey and wastewater measures of consumption generally declined over the period, yet rates of most alcohol-related harms — including ambulance attendances — increased substantially. If reported drinking is falling while harms climb, either the surveys are missing the drinking, or what drinking remains is riskier. The authors flag real doubts about relying on population surveys to track consumption at all. The caveat: these are aggregated population-level indicators — nothing ties a harm to an individual presentation, and the different measures are hard to align. For crews, this is the weekend night-time demand curve, quantified.

Paper four — Paramedicine. An exploratory qualitative study — a single focus group of undergraduate paramedic students — on how they experienced an interprofessional simulation event alongside nursing, medical and emergency service students. Two themes came out: negotiating professional authority, and preparing for collaborative practice. Students said the simulation challenged their assumptions about hierarchy and role identity, and reshaped how they thought about patient advocacy, handover and assertive communication. One focus group at one institution — treat it as texture, not proof. But the observation that siloed, discipline-specific handover habits show up even in students will feel familiar to anyone who's precepted a new graduate.

Quick recap. One — in PEA arrests, witnessed status and prehospital ROSC predict good outcomes, but rhythm conversion only helps when the cause is cardiac. Two — a drone AI system measured four vital signs and triaged casualties from the air, in a controlled pilot. Three — Australian alcohol harms keep rising even as measured consumption falls. Four — interprofessional simulation pushed paramedic students to rethink authority, advocacy and handover.

That's the roundup for Tuesday the twentieth of October. Papers on the dashboard. Back tomorrow.
