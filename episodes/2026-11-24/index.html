Transcript

Two papers to get through today, both from Resuscitation Plus. Paper one — a capnography method for measuring ventilation rate during CPR agrees well with independent detectors, but found no link between ventilation rate and return of spontaneous circulation. Paper two — a European survey showing AED data retrieval is a mess, with freely available software for fewer than half the devices.

Paper one — Resuscitation Plus. A cohort study from a physician-staffed mobile emergency unit, eighty-three CPR segments from seventy-five out-of-hospital cardiac arrest attempts. The question: can you measure ventilation rate reliably during chest compressions using capnography alone? The team built a breath-detection framework based on capnographic valleys with artefact rejection, then checked it against two independent detectors. Agreement was moderate to good — correlations between zero point seven two and zero point eight two — but individual rates could differ by up to about seven breaths per minute. So the method is reproducible in principle, but the detection algorithm you use matters. On outcomes, they found no association between ventilation rate and return of spontaneous circulation. Important caveat, though — that analysis was exploratory and only powered to detect a fairly large effect, so a real benefit of tighter rate control can't be ruled out. Capnography is standard kit across Australian ambulances, and ten breaths a minute is the universal target once the airway's secured — so crews watching the waveform should know the number itself comes with some wiggle room, depending on how it's measured.

Paper two — also Resuscitation Plus. A cross-sectional survey of sixty automated external defibrillators from twenty-four manufacturers across Italy, Slovenia, Spain and Norway. The focus wasn't shocking — it was getting the data off the device afterwards. Transitional interfaces, mostly removable storage and standard USB cables, were the most common at about forty-two per cent of devices. Twenty-two per cent were still on legacy tech — infrared ports, proprietary serial cables. Only about thirty-seven per cent had modern interfaces. Software was the bigger problem: freely available for just forty-three per cent of devices, paid software needed for twenty-seven per cent, and thirteen per cent required going through an authorised distributor. The limitation: it's a descriptive hardware survey with no link to patient outcomes, and the device mix on Australian shelves will differ. But the point lands — defibrillator data feeds arrest registries and quality improvement everywhere, and this kind of fragmentation makes that harder than it should be.

Quick recap. One — capnography can measure CPR ventilation rate reproducibly, but no link to ROSC in this exploratory analysis. Two — AED data retrieval across Europe is fragmented, with free software for fewer than half of sixty devices.
