    New England Journal of Medicine · 18 May 2026 · RCT · Supports current practice

    Multicenter randomized trial comparing prehospital type O whole blood resuscitation to standard care in trauma patients with hemorrhage.

Transcript

No new papers today, so one from the archive. The New England Journal of Medicine ran a pragmatic, multicentre, cluster-randomised phase three trial of prehospital resuscitation with type O whole blood for trauma. Forty-four air medical bases were assigned in one-month blocks to carry up to two units of whole blood, or as-indicated blood components — plasma, red cells, or both — for injured patients in haemorrhagic shock. Six hundred and ninety-five patients got whole blood and two hundred and ninety-eight got components in the primary analysis. The headline: whole blood did not beat components. Thirty-day mortality was twenty-five point nine per cent with whole blood against twenty point five per cent with components — an adjusted odds ratio of one point two four, with a confidence interval crossing one. A substudy found storage age didn't matter either: blood stored fifteen to twenty-one days performed the same as blood under fourteen days old.

Why it matters on the road — prehospital blood is still limited to a handful of Australian services, and this trial is a useful reality check. The more complete product isn't automatically better; early component therapy did the job here.

The main caveat: it's a US air medical setting with a cluster design that left the groups badly unbalanced — seven hundred and fifteen versus three hundred and five — which saps statistical power.
