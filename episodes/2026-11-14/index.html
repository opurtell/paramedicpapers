Transcript

Ten new papers today — here are the top four. Paper one — advanced airways didn't improve survival in traumatic cardiac arrest. Paper two — paramedics miss about half of seizures, and most non-convulsive ones. Paper three — a sepsis flag at dispatch got patients antibiotics half an hour sooner. Paper four — three per cent of callers generate a fifth of all ambulance calls.

Paper one — Prehospital Emergency Care. A nationwide registry cohort from Japan, seven thousand two hundred and seventeen adults with traumatic out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, using the All-Japan Utstein registry from twenty twenty-one to twenty twenty-three. The question: does a prehospital advanced airway — a supraglottic airway or intubation — improve outcomes? Thirty-day survival was one point eight per cent with an advanced airway versus one point four per cent in matched controls, and favourable neurological outcome was nought point two versus nought point four per cent. The clever part is the analysis — time-dependent propensity matching, which corrects for resuscitation time bias, the trap where crews who secure an airway are simply the ones with a longer arrest to work on. Even with that correction, no significant benefit and no timing effect. The caveat: survival in traumatic arrest is so rare that a real effect could still be hiding in those confidence intervals. Airway management is still standard care across Australian services — but this is a reminder the evidence it changes outcomes is thin.

Paper two — Seizure. A scoping review, eighteen studies of paramedics and ambulance nurses pulled from over twelve hundred citations. Where studies measured diagnostic performance, sensitivity sat between fifty-four and fifty-eight per cent — specificity was high, over ninety-five per cent. So when crews do call it a seizure, they're usually right — but roughly half of seizures go unrecognised. Non-convulsive seizures were the hardest of all, missed in between fifty-three and eighty-one per cent of cases. The caveat: the reference standards varied from study to study, so pooling is shaky. The takeaway for crews is that an absent convulsion isn't an absence of seizure — it's worth keeping non-convulsive status in mind for unexplained altered consciousness.

Paper three — the Scandinavian Journal of Trauma, Resuscitation and Emergency Medicine. A Norwegian registry study, five hundred and twenty-seven sepsis admissions at one hospital, linking ambulance and hospital records. When the emergency dispatch centre flagged the call as suspected sepsis, patients reached antibiotics thirty-one minutes sooner after arriving at the emergency department — adjusted for age, sex and triage priority. The caveat: only thirty-one patients actually carried that dispatch flag, so the estimate rests on a very small subgroup with a wide confidence interval. But the signal makes sense — what call-taking decides upstream shapes what the hospital does downstream, and recognising sepsis at dispatch is squarely an ambulance job.

Paper four — BMC Emergency Medicine. A Danish observational cohort, over four hundred and forty thousand calls to one dispatch centre between twenty eighteen and twenty twenty-five. Three per cent of callers — people ringing five or more times in twelve months — generated nearly twenty per cent of all calls. In the highest-volume group, alcohol, intoxication, overdose and suicidal presentations joined the most common contact causes. Those calls also resolved differently: nearly a quarter ended with telephone advice instead of an ambulance, and non-conveyance after dispatch reached twenty-seven per cent. The caveat: it's a single region, and the five-call definition may not match other systems. For anyone working around frequent-caller programs, this puts hard numbers on the load.

Quick recap. One — advanced airways showed no survival benefit in traumatic cardiac arrest in a Japanese registry of over seven thousand patients. Two — paramedic seizure recognition is patchy, and non-convulsive seizures are missed most. Three — a sepsis flag at dispatch meant antibiotics thirty-one minutes sooner. Four — three per cent of callers drove a fifth of all calls.
