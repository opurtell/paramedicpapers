Transcript

Three papers today. Paper one — a peer-support training program for first responders looked feasible and well received, but almost nobody actually used it afterwards. Paper two — a systematic review of shift-worker nutrition finds the evidence base barely touches ambulance crews. Paper three — a survey of prehospital crews finds real knowledge gaps in paediatric anaphylaxis, especially positioning and auto-injector use.

Paper one — Journal of Traumatic Stress. A single-service feasibility study, twenty-one first responders who completed a three-day course in trauma risk management, or TRiM — a structured peer support program. The training itself went well: ninety to ninety-four percent rated it highly relevant, and there were solid improvements in perceived capability, knowledge of the referral process, risk evaluation and professional boundaries. But here's the number that jumps out at me — at both the two-month and six-month follow-ups, formal use of the TRiM procedure was minimal. The team learned the tools, then barely used them. Australian emergency services already run peer support programs along these lines, so the model fits — but the lesson is that training alone doesn't change practice. Without implementation supports afterwards, the course is just three good days. Worth noting it's small — twenty-seven registered, twenty-one completed — and it was a fire service rather than ambulance.

Paper two — Journal of Human Nutrition and Dietetics. A systematic review of nutrition interventions for shift workers, with first responders as a pre-specified subgroup. The search pulled in nearly seven thousand articles — six thousand nine hundred and nine — and just forty-three made the cut. Most were crossover randomised trials or observational studies. Forty percent involved nurses and healthcare professionals, and only nine percent covered first responders at all — firefighters and police. So for ambulance work, the evidence is thin. Shift work is linked to chronic disease, and diet is one of the modifiable levers, but almost none of this research has been done on crews like you. I'd treat this one as context rather than practice change — the review's real finding is the gap itself.

Paper three — Iranian Journal of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology. A cross-sectional survey of three hundred and twenty-two prehospital personnel — paramedics, EMTs and physicians — on paediatric anaphylaxis management. The good news: about eighty-two percent recognised adrenaline as first-line treatment, and eighty-one percent knew the intramuscular route — squarely in Australian scope, that one. The gaps are what matter: only about half identified all three diagnostic criteria, only half knew correct patient positioning, and just fifty-one percent got auto-injector use right. Paediatric anaphylaxis is a low-frequency, high-stakes call, so those gaps are a good prompt to check yourself against your own guidelines. The big caveat: it's a self-reported survey from a different EMS and training system, with no link to actual patient outcomes — treat it as a prompt, not a benchmark.

Quick recap. One — TRiM peer-support training worked well in the room but barely got used afterwards; implementation support is the missing piece. Two — shift-worker nutrition research exists, but only a sliver of it involves first responders. Three — roughly half of surveyed prehospital crews missed the mark on paediatric anaphylaxis: diagnosis, positioning and auto-injectors were the weak spots.

That's the roundup for Sunday the fourth of October. Papers on the dashboard. Back tomorrow.
