Transcript

Three papers to get through today. Paper one — a real-time ventilation feedback device lifted guideline-adherent paediatric tidal volumes from sixteen per cent of breaths to eighty-seven. Paper two — a consensus statement says traumatic brain injury in first responders is a substantial but underrecognised occupational burden, with no consistent pathways for recognition or return to duty. Paper three — a four-hour joint training course lifted paramedic confidence in behavioural emergencies, but didn't shift stigma.

Paper one — the journal Paediatrics. A randomised crossover simulation study, one hundred and twenty-three EMS clinicians across five agencies in the United States. Each clinician ventilated a simulated three-year-old in respiratory arrest, with and without real-time feedback from a ventilation feedback device, in randomised order. Without feedback, guideline-recommended tidal volumes landed in just sixteen per cent of breaths. With feedback, that jumped to eighty-seven per cent. Ventilation rate adherence improved from sixty-five to nearly eighty-nine per cent, and mask leak under thirty per cent improved from eighty to eighty-eight. So the baseline was poor — clinicians rarely met paediatric ventilation guidelines unaided. The big caveat is that this is a manikin, not a child, and there are no patient outcomes. Real paediatric airways are less forgiving. But bagging a child is core practice on Australian ambulances, and the age-based targets here are the same ones, so a device that moves adherence this much deserves attention.

Paper two — The Journal of Head Trauma Rehabilitation. A consensus statement and themed issue on traumatic brain injury in first responders — police, fire and ambulance personnel. The headline: TBI, including concussion, is a common but underrecognised occupational injury, and there are no consistent pathways for recognition, reporting, assessment, rehabilitation, or safe return to duty. The multidisciplinary group put out twenty-eight recommendations covering recognition, early assessment, return to duty, mental health, prevention and organisational policy. The authors concede the first-responder-specific evidence base remains limited — this is a framework, not proven practice. Still, the links between TBI history, psychological health and occupational functioning will look familiar to paramedics here, and return-to-duty structures vary widely across Australian services.

Paper three — BMC Medical Education. A city-wide pre-post study from Taiwan: a four-hour interprofessional course on behavioural emergencies, training paramedics alongside police, fire, nursing and mental health staff. Seven hundred and fifty attendees. In the three hundred and ninety-five paired analyses, self-efficacy rose from three point one six to three point five four — a solid effect size — and knowledge scores improved as well. But stigma toward behavioural emergencies ticked up slightly, and substance-use stigma didn't move at all. The caveats are real: no control group, immediate self-report only, and a different emergency system. Confidence after a course isn't behaviour on the road. Still, joint training across agencies is a live debate for Australian services, so the pattern is worth knowing.

Quick recap. One — real-time feedback lifted guideline-adherent paediatric tidal volumes from sixteen per cent of breaths to eighty-seven. Two — a consensus statement calls TBI in first responders a hidden occupational burden and lays out twenty-eight recommendations. Three — joint training in Taiwan lifted confidence in behavioural emergencies but left stigma unchanged.

That's the roundup for Wednesday the twenty-first of October. Papers on the dashboard. Back tomorrow.
