Transcript

Two papers today. Paper one — prehospital stroke triage tools usually look cost-effective, but keeping them running is the hard part. Paper two — Danish data puts a number on how rarely crews attend an actual prehospital birth: about three in every ten thousand missions.

Paper one — Journal of Evaluation in Clinical Practice. A systematic review of the health economics and implementation evidence for prehospital stroke triage tools — mobile stroke units, telemedicine, plus a few emerging options like biomarkers and an AI-based microwave device. From nearly six and a half thousand screened studies, thirty-one made the cut. Eleven of them reported positive cost-effectiveness. But evidence on keeping these programs running over the long term was thin. The recurring barriers were unglamorous: how complex the innovation is, role delineation, whether it fits existing workflows, IT infrastructure, and cost. That's the investment debate in one line — the economics can stack up, yet programs stall on implementation. Worth a grain of salt, though: this was a narrative synthesis of very different study designs, and only thirteen of the thirty-one studies even reported economic outcomes. Here in Australia, mobile stroke units and prehospital telemedicine run in only a handful of regions, so this informs the investment debate more than everyday practice.

Paper two — Acta Anaesthesiologica Scandinavica. A cross-sectional review of eight years of prehospital records in a Danish region of one point two million people. Out of more than one point one million ambulance dispatches, three and a half thousand were childbirth-related — about zero point three per cent. Ambulance staff assisted with just three hundred and ten out-of-hospital deliveries — zero point zero three per cent of all missions — and performed delaying obstetric manoeuvres in ten further complex cases while the mother was rushed in. Most births happened without complications. The number that jumps out is how little hands-on birth practice most crews ever get — a universal problem, not a Danish one. The caveat: it's a single region, with manual record review, and no maternal or newborn outcomes beyond the EMS encounter.

Quick recap. One — prehospital stroke triage tools are mostly cost-effective on paper, but complexity, workflow fit and costs keep sinking sustained implementation. Two — an unplanned prehospital birth is vanishingly rare: three hundred and ten assisted deliveries across eight years and more than a million dispatches.

That's the roundup for Tuesday the sixth of October. Papers on the dashboard. Back tomorrow.
