  • Electrical capture uncommon in prehospital transcutaneous pacing

    Prehospital Emergency Care · 5 Nov 2026 · Cohort · Hypothesis-generating

    A US EMS agency reviewed continuous monitor ECGs from 56 patients given prehospital transcutaneous pacing between 2017 and 2024 and found electrical capture in only 32%, often intermittent, with clinician-verbalised pulses coinciding with capture in just 15% of instances.

Transcript

Four papers today. Paper one — prehospital transcutaneous pacing achieved electrical capture in only a third of patients, and crews could rarely tell when it was working. Paper two — counted drip rates drift badly from target, and the error is mostly human. Paper three — two thirds of military prehospital transfusions happened outside guideline criteria. Paper four — START triage knowledge in paramedic students had nothing to do with their decision-making style.

Paper one — Prehospital Emergency Care. A retrospective cohort study of fifty-six patients who received prehospital transcutaneous pacing from a US ambulance service between twenty seventeen and twenty twenty-four. The researchers went back through the monitors' continuous ECG recordings. Electrical capture — a widened QRS with a distinct T wave after the pacing spike — turned up in just eighteen of the fifty-six, that's thirty-two percent. And even among those, complete capture occurred only about twenty-seven percent of the time they were pacing. Blood pressure told the same story — a median rise of twenty-two millimetres of mercury with capture, essentially nothing without it. The number that jumps out at me is this: crews verbalised a palpable pulse twenty-six times during pacing, and only four of those — fifteen percent — actually coincided with electrical capture. So the routine bedside signs were unreliable for telling whether the pacing was doing anything. Transcutaneous pacing is scoped to only a handful of Australian services — most don't carry it — but for the services that do, that's a strong argument for verifying capture properly rather than trusting the obvious signs. Caveats: a single US agency, small numbers, and capture judged from monitor recordings rather than patient-centred outcomes.

Paper two — also Prehospital Emergency Care. A controlled laboratory simulation with fifty-five paramedics, each setting nine drip rates by eye on a standardised gravity set. Overall flow errors ranged from about forty-five percent below target to nearly six percent above. The imprecision was mostly human — counting drops, not the equipment. Whole-number target rates beat fractional ones, and one drop per second beat everything else — absolute error around thirty-one percent versus forty-eight. That's under laboratory conditions, too — no moving vehicle, no different tubing — so the field would likely be worse. Gravity sets are standard kit on Australian ambulances, so this lands squarely in everyday practice. If the rate actually matters for what you're running, a whole-number target counted steadily is the safer bet.

Paper three — Transfusion. A multicentre retrospective study from the French military health service, looking at two hundred and six trauma casualties evacuated by forward aeromedical teams in the Sahel between twenty fifteen and twenty twenty-three. Sixty-four casualties received prehospital transfusion — eighty-nine units of blood products in total. Meeting the guideline criteria did predict who needed blood — roughly three times the relative risk — but sixty-five percent of the patients actually transfused didn't meet the criteria at all. Heart rate above one hundred and ten, a shock index above one, penetrating or pelvic trauma, and free fluid on FAST ultrasound were all more common in the transfused group. Median transfusion rate was about twenty-five mils a minute. Prehospital blood remains limited to a few Australian services, and this comes out of a military evacuation chain rather than a civilian one — so it's context for the transfusion-trigger debate, not a change to current practice.

Paper four — Disaster Medicine and Public Health Preparedness. A cross-sectional questionnaire study of one hundred and twenty-one paramedic students, looking at whether intolerance of uncertainty and decision-making styles tracked with theoretical START triage knowledge. Mean knowledge score was about seventy, with a wide spread. The association with decision-making style? None — no subscale reached significance. I'd file this one as low-stakes: it's a paper-based knowledge test from a single training program, with self-reported psychosocial measures, and it never tested triage performance in a scenario. Not much here that transfers to the road.

Quick recap. One — transcutaneous pacing achieved electrical capture in only a third of patients, and verbalised pulses rarely matched. Two — counted drip rates missed their targets by up to forty-five percent, and the error is mostly human. Three — sixty-five percent of military prehospital transfusions fell outside guideline criteria, though the classic shock signs did flag who needed blood. Four — START triage knowledge in students didn't track with their decision-making style at all.

That's the roundup for Thursday the fifth of November. All four papers on the dashboard. Back tomorrow.
