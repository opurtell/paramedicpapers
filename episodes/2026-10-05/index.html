Transcript

Two papers today. Paper one — a six-week online stroke education program roughly tripled stroke knowledge, warning-sign awareness and intent to call an ambulance among high-risk residents in China. Paper two — interviews with resuscitation nurses in Iran map out how environment, cognition and team stress pile up to degrade CPR performance.

Paper one — Journal of Education and Health Promotion. A randomised controlled trial, one hundred and six high-risk residents in Huanggang City, China, split fifty-three per group. The intervention group got a six-week online stroke education program; the control group got usual health education. After the program, the intervention group showed significantly bigger gains across the board — warning-sign awareness, stroke knowledge, and intention to call emergency services, each around three times more likely to improve, with adjusted odds ratios between roughly three-point-three and three-point-eight. Why it matters: most of the delay in stroke care happens before the ambulance is even called. Anything that gets high-risk people recognising warning signs and dialling faster shortens the whole chain — and that first link is squarely what paramedics see on the road when the stroke patient arrives six hours late. The caveats are real, though. It's a small, single-city sample, the outcomes were self-reported through interviews, and it measured intent, not actual emergency calls or door-to-treatment times. So treat it as hypothesis-generating, but the model — short digital education modules delivered through primary care — is scalable and cheap.

Paper two — BMC Emergency Medicine. A qualitative study, twenty-one resuscitation team members in Kermanshah, Iran, all with at least two years' experience and five or more arrests in the past three months. Semi-structured interviews, conventional content analysis. What came out is a nested four-layer model: physical and environmental threats form the outer layer, and those trigger cognitive and motivational barriers — those interact with psychological and ethical stressors at the team level — and socio-cognitive challenges extend it all beyond the hospital walls. In plain terms: a chaotic room, a fatigued brain and a stressed team feed into each other, and resuscitation quality degrades as a system, not as one bad cog. Australian crews will recognise the pattern immediately, even though this data comes from in-hospital emergency department teams in a very different health system — nothing here speaks to prehospital arrests directly. And the themes were never tested against patient outcomes, so it's context, not evidence.

Quick recap. One — a six-week online stroke education program in China tripled knowledge, awareness and ambulance-calling intent among high-risk residents. Two — a qualitative model from Iran shows how environment, cognition and team stressors interact to compromise CPR performance.
