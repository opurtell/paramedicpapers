    Emergency Medicine International · 17 Nov 2026 · Systematic review · Context only

    A Finnish-led umbrella review of 20 studies defined the competences paramedics need in out-of-hospital emergency care, organised into five domains: interpersonal and teamwork, cognitive and decision-making, professionalism and ethical, clinical, and situation-specific or high-stress competence.

Transcript

Three papers today. Paper one — a survey of England's air ambulance services finds every one of them can do a prehospital field amputation, but there's little national standardisation. Paper two — an umbrella review maps paramedic competence into five domains. Paper three — Norwegian crews show that running a randomised trial inside an ambulance service is feasible, but operationally demanding.

Paper one — Injury. A cross-sectional survey of fourteen helicopter emergency medical services in England. The question — what does field amputation practice actually look like across those services. Every service could provide the procedure. Twelve of the fourteen — eighty-six per cent — had a clinical practice guideline written specifically for it, but only four had performed one in the last five years, six cases in total. A Gigli saw was the most common instrument for bone disarticulation, used by thirteen services. And none used hospital response teams — the model described in the USA and Korea. Here in Australia, field amputation sits with physician-staffed helicopter teams rather than routine road crews, but the governance questions travel — who trains for it, who signs it off, and how cases get recorded. The big caveat — it's a self-reported survey with only six cases nationally in five years, so the clinical data are thin. The authors' suggestion — a registry of every case.

Paper two — Emergency Medicine International. An umbrella review — a review of reviews — run to Joanna Briggs methodology, with confidence in the findings graded formally. The screeners started from over two thousand two hundred records and ended with eight reviews in the final synthesis. The question — what does competence actually mean for paramedics in out-of-hospital emergency care. Five domains came out. Interpersonal and teamwork. Cognitive and decision-making. Professionalism and ethics. Clinical. And competence across certification levels and in special or high-stress situations. For paramedics here in Australia, that maps closely onto registration standards and the expanding scope of practice debate — competence is clearly more than clinical skills. The limitation — the review describes the domains and stops there. Which educational approaches best build and maintain competence is still an open question.

Paper three — Trials. A cross-sectional survey of sixty-seven ambulance personnel in Norway — ninety-six per cent of those invited — with linked process data from the PreMeFen randomised trial across eighteen months. The question — what does it actually take to run a trial inside an ambulance service. Motivation started high, a median of eight out of ten, but slipped to seven over the trial. Across three hundred and thirty-eight patient inclusions there were ninety-four protocol deviations — twenty-eight per cent — mostly early in the trial, with missing measurements the commonest cause. Ninety per cent felt well informed, but half found the communication excessive. Despite all that, ninety per cent said they'd take part in future research. One regional service, self-reported survey — I'd take that with a grain of salt. The takeaway for Australian services running prehospital trials — train well, support crews continuously, and go easy on the emails.

Quick recap. One — every English air ambulance can do a field amputation, but six cases in five years and little standardisation. Two — paramedic competence comes in five domains, and only one of them is clinical. Three — ambulance trials are feasible but demanding — motivation fades, deviations cluster early, communication can be too much.
