Papers in this episode

  • Nursing-home arrests more often shockable, linked to early AED

    Resuscitation Plus · 1 Nov 2026 · Registry · Hypothesis-generating

    A Japanese population-based registry of 2,478 medical-origin OHCA cases found an initial shockable rhythm in 36.3% of nursing-home arrests versus 7.1% at home (adjusted OR 12.35), with 97% of shockable nursing-home patients receiving an AED shock but only 0.8% surviving with good neurological outcome.

  • Bystanders describe heavy emotional toll of doing CPR

    Resuscitation Plus · 1 Nov 2026 · Qualitative · Hypothesis-generating

    A Scottish qualitative study of 11 bystanders who performed CPR in an OHCA found a substantial emotional impact — worst when the patient died or was known to them — with themes of being compelled to help but unprepared, and identified better emotional preparation in CPR training and access to a knowledgeable independent person as ways to reduce it.

  • NZ paramedics breached patient rights more often than nurses

    Whitireia Journal of Nursing, Health and Social Services · 1 Nov 2026 · Cross-sectional · Context only

    A New Zealand descriptive analysis of Health and Disability Commissioner decisions from 2014–2024 found nine paramedic and 100 nurse Code of Rights breaches, but per 1,000 practitioners paramedics were three times more likely to breach than nurses and at higher severity, with Right 4 (appropriate standard of care) most common in both professions.

  • Drone triage feasible but unproven in real MCIs

    Scandinavian Journal of Trauma, Resuscitation and Emergency Medicine · 1 Nov 2026 · Systematic review · Context only

    A scoping review of 20 studies on UAV-assisted remote triage in mass-casualty and prehospital care found technical feasibility of remote vital-sign acquisition is demonstrated, but only one randomised comparison reached effectiveness — UAV-assisted triage took about 3.5 minutes longer with identical accuracy — and no study validated remote triage in real-world responses.

Transcript

Six new papers today — here are the top four. Paper one — nursing home arrests were far more likely to show a shockable rhythm, likely because AEDs are close at hand. Paper two — bystanders describe a heavy emotional toll from doing CPR. Paper three — in New Zealand, paramedics breached patient rights at three times the rate of nurses per thousand practitioners. Paper four — drone-assisted triage works in trials but has never been proven in a real mass casualty.

Paper one — Resuscitation Plus. A population-based registry cohort from Japan — two thousand four hundred and seventy-eight cardiac arrests of medical origin between twenty sixteen and twenty twenty-four, split by arrest location. Just over thirty-six per cent of nursing home arrests showed an initial shockable rhythm, against about seven per cent at a private home and thirty-three per cent elsewhere. After adjustment, a shockable rhythm was more than twelve times more likely in a nursing home than at a home address. And ninety-seven per cent of those shockable nursing home cases received an AED shock. The authors think earlier AED access explains much of the gap — the rhythm is found sooner after collapse, before it deteriorates. The sting is in the outcomes: despite all those shockable rhythms, one-month survival with good neurological function was lowest in nursing homes, at under one per cent. It's descriptive data, so causation isn't proven. But residential aged care is a common arrest location here in Australia, and it's a good reminder that early rhythm detection through prompt AED use changes what crews walk in on.

Paper two — also Resuscitation Plus. A qualitative study from Scotland — semi-structured interviews with eleven bystanders who performed CPR on someone in cardiac arrest. Most knew the person — family, a teammate, a neighbour. Four themes came out: the emotional impact, juggling multiple roles in the moment, feeling compelled to help but unprepared, and wanting to talk to the right person afterwards. The impact was worst when the patient died or was known to the bystander. The authors point to better emotional preparation in CPR training, and the chance to speak with an independent person who knows cardiac arrest. Small, self-selected sample recruited through social media — take it as a signal, not a measurement. Still, it's a pattern experienced crews will recognise — the bystander left standing on the footpath needs support too, and the training could prepare them for that as well as the compressions.

Paper three — the Whitireia Journal of Nursing, Health and Social Services. A descriptive analysis of Health and Disability Commissioner decisions in New Zealand across a decade — one hundred and nine upheld breaches of the patient rights code, nine by paramedics and one hundred by nurses. The raw counts favour nursing, but per thousand practitioners, paramedics were three times more likely to breach the code, and breached at a higher severity. Right four — the right to services of an appropriate standard — was the most common breach in both professions. The caveat is obvious: nine paramedic cases is a tiny number, and published decisions only capture what gets reported and upheld. The authors frame it against underreporting culture — fear and punitive structures keep errors quiet. Error reporting culture is a live issue across Australian services too, so this one is context worth having.

Paper four — the Scandinavian Journal of Trauma, Resuscitation and Emergency Medicine. A scoping review of twenty studies on drone-assisted remote triage in mass casualty and prehospital care. Capturing heart rate, respiratory rate, temperature, oxygen saturation and consciousness from the air is technically feasible. But only one study reached the question that matters — a randomised comparison where drone-assisted triage took about three and a half minutes longer, with identical accuracy. There's no validated remote triage standard, and no validation in a real-world response. Drones are being trialled in Australia, so worth knowing exactly where the evidence stops.

Quick recap. One — nursing home arrests are far more often shockable, likely thanks to early AED access, though survival there remains poor. Two — bystander CPR takes a real emotional toll, and better preparation and support could soften it. Three — per practitioner, New Zealand paramedics breached patient rights more often, and more severely, than nurses. Four — drone triage is feasible in trials but unproven in the real world.
