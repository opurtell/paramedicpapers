Papers in this episode

  • Transport ventilation during CPR feasible in pilot

    BMC Emergency Medicine · 28 Oct 2026 · Other · Hypothesis-generating

    A Thai exploratory pilot compared transport mechanical ventilation (HAMILTON-T1, volume-controlled) with bag-valve ventilation during CPR in adult OHCA and found no significant differences in PCO2, pH, pO2, sustained ROSC or 28-day survival, with mechanical ventilation operationally feasible.

  • Longer on-scene intervals linked to 24-hour mortality

    Archives of Academic Emergency Medicine · 28 Oct 2026 · Cohort · Hypothesis-generating

    A Thai retrospective cohort of Code Red ALS activations found on-scene intervals of 21-25 minutes were associated with higher odds of 24-hour mortality compared with 5 minutes or less (adjusted OR 5.61), and intervals of 20 minutes or longer came with 321.7 fewer expected survival minutes.

  • Tele-paramedics matched physicians in MCI drill

    Healthcare · 28 Oct 2026 · Simulation · Context only

    In a German mass-casualty simulation, tele-consult support delivered by experienced paramedics performed similarly to tele-physician support and on-site physician care on indicated treatments addressed (51% vs 56-57%) and analgesia provision (~60%), both far ahead of EMT-only care (8% and 7%).

  • What drives lay community first responders in Denmark

    BMC Emergency Medicine · 28 Oct 2026 · Qualitative · Context only

    An interpretive phenomenological study of ten Danish lay community first responders — part of a 60,000-volunteer national system, about 70% without healthcare backgrounds — identified four themes: a sense of duty, acting under uncertainty as a civilian-turned-responder, navigating relational roles with crews, and carrying personal identity into crisis.

Transcript

Six new papers today — here are the top four. Paper one — transport mechanical ventilation during CPR looks feasible, with physiology comparable to bag-valve. Paper two — longer on-scene intervals were linked to higher twenty-four-hour mortality. Paper three — tele-paramedics matched physicians as remote consultants in a mass-casualty drill. Paper four — Danish lay community first responders are driven by duty, meaning and community.

Paper one — BMC Emergency Medicine. An exploratory pilot from Thailand. Forty-one adults in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. For the first six months crews ventilated with a bag-valve during CPR, then switched to a transport ventilator in volume-controlled mode. On the numbers that matter — carbon dioxide, pH and oxygen levels — there were no significant differences between the groups. Sustained return of spontaneous circulation and twenty-eight-day survival were similar too. Mechanical ventilation was operationally feasible, despite implementation challenges. Two big caveats. It was a pilot, never powered to detect outcome differences. And the groups were unequal and sequential rather than randomised — twenty-nine bag-valve patients against twelve on the ventilator. So this is hypothesis-generating, not practice-changing. Transport ventilators for ongoing CPR are an intensive-care capability on Australian ambulances rather than standard kit, but for advanced-tier crews running a prolonged arrest, this is exactly their question.

Paper two — Archives of Academic Emergency Medicine. A retrospective cohort of two thousand three hundred and twenty-one advanced life support activations in Thailand, all asking the same question — does time on scene affect survival? Non-survivors had a longer median on-scene interval — twelve minutes against nine. After adjustment, an on-scene interval of twenty-one to twenty-five minutes was associated with more than five-fold higher odds of twenty-four-hour mortality, compared with five minutes or less. Overall twenty-four-hour mortality was about five per cent. The catch — prolonged scene time may reflect necessary care in sicker patients rather than avoidable delay, and an observational design can't untangle that. Still, scene time is one of the few modifiable numbers in a response, and this is the kind of result that feeds quality-improvement reviews.

Paper three — Healthcare. A German mass-casualty simulation — one hundred and fifty-one simulated patients across seven treatment tents. Four setups. An on-site physician. EMTs alone. Remote physician support. And remote support delivered by experienced paramedics. The headline — tele-paramedics and tele-physicians performed about the same, and both sat close to the on-site physician. Indicated treatments addressed — fifty-one per cent for tele-paramedics, fifty-seven for tele-physicians, fifty-six on-site, against just eight per cent for EMTs alone. Analgesia told the same story — roughly sixty per cent with remote support, seven per cent without. Time to first treatment order didn't differ. It was a drill with simulated patients, so real-disaster performance is untested. But for any Australian service designing mass-casualty telehealth support, the message is clear — the remote expert doesn't have to be a physician.

Paper four — BMC Emergency Medicine again, and a qualitative one. Denmark runs a national community first responder system with more than sixty thousand volunteers, about seventy per cent of them without a healthcare background. Researchers interviewed ten of those lay responders. Four themes came out — a sense of duty, acting under uncertainty as a civilian-turned-responder, navigating relationships with professionals and peers, and carrying their own identity into crisis. Ten volunteers recruited through social media, so nothing generalisable here in the numbers sense. But community responder schemes exist in parts of Australia in smaller forms, and for crews working alongside them, this is a useful window into what motivates the person arriving before you do.

Quick recap. One — mechanical ventilation during CPR was feasible, with physiology similar to bag-valve. Two — on-scene intervals over twenty minutes were linked to higher twenty-four-hour mortality, though sicker patients may drive that link. Three — tele-paramedics matched physicians as remote consultants in a mass-casualty drill. Four — Danish lay first responders act from duty, meaning and community.
