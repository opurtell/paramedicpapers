Transcript

Three papers today, and two of them are big ones — the PITSTOP trials from Canada, testing whether starting sepsis treatment in the ambulance actually saves lives. Paper one — liberal fluids in the ambulance made no difference to mortality in suspected sepsis. Paper two — antibiotics given in the ambulance didn't either. Paper three — at a mountain paragliding festival, most injuries happened during landing.

Paper one — JAMA. A multicentre factorial randomised controlled trial across four Ontario paramedic services, sixteen hundred and forty-six adults with suspected sepsis, median age seventy-six. Half were randomised to liberal fluids — up to two litres of normal saline regardless of blood pressure — and half to usual care, fluids only when the systolic dropped below ninety. The liberal group ended up with a median of fifteen hundred mills, the usual-care group five hundred. Hospital mortality was eighteen point zero percent versus eighteen point three — no real difference, and all seventeen secondary outcomes looked the same too. The caveat: the entry criteria — fever plus a systolic under a hundred — excluded the sickest patients, and even usual care gave most of them a bolus, so this tested a fairly narrow window. Still, it supports the more conservative fluid approach most Australian services already take — more saline in the ambulance didn't buy anything.

Paper two — also JAMA, the sister trial from the same program. Same four Ontario services, two thousand and forty-eight adults with suspected sepsis, this time randomised to one gram of intramuscular ceftriaxone in the ambulance or a placebo. Hospital mortality: eighteen point zero percent with the antibiotic versus eighteen point four with placebo. Again nothing across the seventeen secondary outcomes. The telling detail is timing — for most patients, hospital antibiotics came within about four hours of paramedic arrival, so the ambulance dose barely moved the clock. Ceftriaxone is standard kit across nearly all Australian services, so this question was live here — and the answer is that the race to the first dose mattered less than getting these patients to definitive care quickly.

Paper three — Wilderness and Environmental Medicine. A retrospective look at records from a two-day mountain paragliding festival in Turkey. Forty-seven hundred and sixteen flights, thirteen injuries. Ten of the thirteen happened during landing — just one at takeoff — and lower-limb trauma dominated. Eight patients went by ground ambulance, and one pilot who died needed a helicopter-assisted recovery. It's a single event with small numbers, so treat it as context rather than evidence — but for anyone covering a festival or flight-sport event, the pattern is clear: the transition phases, especially landing, are where the injuries happen, and that's where the response point belongs.

Quick recap. One — liberal ambulance fluids didn't cut sepsis mortality, eighteen percent either way. Two — ambulance ceftriaxone didn't either, because hospital antibiotics weren't far behind. Three — at a paragliding festival, three-quarters of the injuries happened at landing.
