Transcript

Seven new papers today — here are the top four. Paper one — a one-hour airway assistant course nearly halved intubation time in a simulated regurgitating airway. Paper two — Japanese registry data shows the odds of on-scene ROSC drop off a cliff after ten minutes. Paper three — adding police and fire to cardiac arrest dispatch cut modelled response times by more than four minutes. Paper four — social support weakens the link between workplace violence and burnout.

Paper one — Prehospital Emergency Care. An Australian pilot simulation study. Twelve qualified paramedics, split into two groups of six, each paired with an intensive care paramedic, intubating a continuously regurgitating manikin — the SALAD setup. The airway assistants in one group had completed a one-hour airway assistant training course; the others hadn't. Median intubation time was fifty-three seconds in the trained group versus a hundred and two and a half seconds in the untrained group — a statistically significant difference with a very large effect size. There were no oesophageal intubations in either group. The experience or age of the assistant or the intubator didn't predict time. Now — twelve people and a manikin, so it's hypothesis-generating: no patient outcomes, and the authors want larger studies. But the assistant role exists on every two-paramedic response here in Australia, and intubation is standard kit across Australian services — so a one-hour course that halves intubation time in a filthy airway is worth a proper trial on patients. That's the takeaway for crews.

Paper two — Journal of Clinical Medicine. A retrospective nationwide cohort using Japanese Utstein data — twenty-two thousand three hundred and eighty-three adults with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest and an initial shockable rhythm, from twenty seventeen to twenty twenty-two. The question: how quickly does on-scene ROSC stop happening? After ten minutes of resuscitation from EMS contact, the probability of subsequent on-scene ROSC was about seven per cent. By twenty minutes, half a per cent. And among the patients who achieved on-scene ROSC at or after ten minutes, favourable neurological outcome at one month — roughly half of those under seventy-five, but only about one in five of those seventy-five and over. It's descriptive data — no causal claims, and the authors are explicit that it doesn't establish age-based rules for stopping or transporting. But it puts hard numbers on a decision crews wrestle with on scene: what ten more minutes of compressions is actually buying.

Paper three — also the Journal of Clinical Medicine. A geospatial simulation from Austria — five hundred modelled cardiac arrest locations, routed against eighteen ambulance stations, twenty-eight police stations and a hundred and twenty-one volunteer fire stations. With ambulances alone, median first-unit response was nine point eight minutes. Add police: five and a half. Add fire: seven point four. Triple dispatch: five point four minutes — a median gain of four point three minutes — and locations reached within ten minutes jumped from just over half to ninety-five per cent. The catch: it's purely modelled. Fixed stations, assumed turnout times, every unit always available. The authors want operational validation before anyone builds policy on it. But the shape of the result is the interesting bit — police arrived first at most locations, fire stations pulled hardest in rural areas, and together they covered the most ground. First-responder co-response to cardiac arrest is a live resourcing question for Australian services too.

Paper four — Healthcare. A cross-sectional survey of two hundred and ten fire-service paramedics in South Korea. Workplace violence exposure was associated with burnout — but indirectly, through post-traumatic stress symptoms. Social support moderated both links: the more support people reported, the weaker the pathway from violence to burnout. Resilience, interestingly, didn't hold up once the statistics were corrected for multiple comparisons. Caveats: cross-sectional self-report, and one of the social-support findings only held after excluding a single influential observation. Still — the takeaway is that social support is something services can actually build, and here it seems to do real protective work.

Quick recap. One — a one-hour airway assistant course halved intubation time in a simulated regurgitating airway. Two — after twenty minutes of shockable-rhythm resuscitation, the chance of on-scene ROSC is around half a per cent. Three — modelled police and fire co-dispatch cut cardiac arrest response times by over four minutes. Four — social support buffers the path from workplace violence to burnout.
