Papers in this episode

  • Updated paediatric traumatic arrest position statement

    Prehospital Emergency Care · 20 Nov 2026 · Guideline / statement · Supports current practice

    A US joint position statement and resource document from NAEMSP, ACS-COT, ACEP and AAP updates guidance on withholding or discontinuing resuscitation in paediatric traumatic out-of-hospital circulatory arrest, prioritising reversible causes, rapid haemorrhage control, least-invasive airway care and transport to a trauma centre when safe and appropriate.

  • Choking arrests: ROSC common, survival still poor

    Resuscitation · 20 Nov 2026 · Registry · Supports current practice

    A Canadian CanROC registry cohort of 105,178 EMS-treated out-of-hospital cardiac arrests (2005–2023) found 1,106 (1.1%) were obviously foreign-body-airway-obstruction-related: prehospital ROSC was far more frequent than in presumed cardiac arrests (62.7% vs 37.8%) but survival to discharge was similar (10.6% vs 9.0%).

  • Traumatic pneumothorax: most EDs don't drain early

    Emergency Medicine Australasia · 20 Nov 2026 · Cohort · Context only

    An Australian two-centre retrospective cohort of 339 ED patients with traumatic pneumothorax found two-thirds were managed conservatively before leaving the ED, and of the 50 who arrived after a prehospital pleural intervention, 27 had chest drains inserted to formalise finger thoracostomy or needle decompression.

  • Studying barriers to trauma triage tool use

    HRB Open Research · 20 Nov 2026 · Other · Context only

    An Irish study protocol describes a qualitative project using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research to explore barriers and enablers to using the HSE trauma triage tool among up to 30 Dublin Fire Brigade paramedics, advanced paramedics, EMTs, firefighters and managers.

Transcript

Five papers on the list today — here are the top four. Paper one — updated international guidance on paediatric traumatic cardiac arrest. Paper two — a Canadian registry showing choking arrests get a pulse back often, but most still don't survive. Paper three — an Australian study finding most traumatic pneumothoraxes leave the emergency department without a drain. Paper four — an Irish protocol studying why trauma triage tools go unused.

Paper one — Prehospital Emergency Care. A joint position statement and resource document from four US bodies, updating guidance on paediatric traumatic cardiac arrest from twenty-fourteen. The recommendations: identify quickly which children might actually benefit from transport to a trauma centre, and treat the reversible causes. That means haemorrhage control with direct pressure, wound packing and tourniquets, the least invasive airway that keeps the child oxygenated, and chest decompression only when there's actual suspicion of a thoracic problem — empiric bilateral decompression is specifically not recommended without it. One point that jumps out at me: compressions and ventilations carry a larger role in paediatric traumatic arrest than in the adult version, so the adult instinct to call it early doesn't transfer. The caveat — this is US consensus guidance, not an Australian one, so withholding and termination decisions still follow local guidelines here. But every intervention it recommends is core Australian paramedic practice.

Paper two — Resuscitation. A retrospective registry cohort from the Canadian Resuscitation Outcomes Consortium, covering one hundred and five thousand one hundred and seventy-eight EMS-treated cardiac arrests between twenty-oh-five and twenty-twenty-three. Just one point one percent — eleven hundred and six cases — were obviously related to foreign body airway obstruction. Here's the headline number: those choking arrests got prehospital return of spontaneous circulation sixty-two point seven percent of the time, against thirty-seven point eight percent for presumed cardiac arrests. But survival to discharge didn't actually differ — ten point six versus nine percent. A pulse back is not the same as a life saved. Within the choking arrests, survival was eighteen point one percent with prehospital return of circulation and zero point eight without, and tracked with younger age, a shockable rhythm, and shorter response times. The classification depends on registry coding rather than a verified cause, so treat the split as exploratory. The takeaway for crews: the airway is the reversible cause, but speed still decides who leaves hospital.

Paper three — Emergency Medicine Australasia. A two-centre Australian retrospective cohort, three hundred and thirty-nine patients aged fourteen and over with traumatic pneumothorax. Two-thirds — sixty-eight point one percent — were initially managed without any drainage before leaving the emergency department. When a drain did go in, pneumothorax size over thirty-five millimetres and a haemothorax were the strong predictors. And nearly a quarter of drained patients had a recorded complication. Worth noting for prehospital crews: fifty patients arrived after a prehospital pleural intervention, and twenty-seven of those had drains inserted to formalise a finger thoracostomy or needle decompression. It's a retrospective look at just two emergency departments and the prehospital subgroup is small, so it's context rather than practice change — but it suggests most of these chests are managed conservatively downstream.

Paper four — HRB Open Research. A qualitative study protocol from Ireland, not results — interviews and focus groups with up to thirty Dublin Fire Brigade responders and managers, exploring why the national trauma triage tool is underused. The factors it targets will sound familiar: time pressure, limited training, resource problems, reluctance to change. Trauma triage tools drive major-trauma destination decisions across Australian services, so the implementation friction is the same here. No findings yet — worth checking back when the results land.

Quick recap. One — updated paediatric traumatic arrest guidance: fix reversible causes, least-invasive airway, no empiric bilateral decompression. Two — choking arrests get a pulse back twice as often as cardiac ones, but survival stays poor. Three — most traumatic pneumothoraxes leave the emergency department without a drain. Four — an Irish protocol examining why trauma triage tools go unused.
