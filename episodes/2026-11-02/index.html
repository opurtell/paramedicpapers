Transcript

One paper today.

Paper one — the International Journal of Nursing Studies Advances. A modified Delphi consensus study, run in the United Kingdom with international representation on the panel. One hundred and four participants registered — doctors, advanced practitioners, paramedics, nurses and academics. They set out to build a consensus-based quality metric for bystander CPR in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, because there's no standardised tool for measuring it right now. Eleven criteria reached consensus, at seventy-five per cent agreement or better. The mechanical ones you'd expect — chest compression rate, depth and recoil — plus contextual factors: dispatcher instruction, time from recognition to the emergency call, and AED use. Every criterion scores dichotomously — done or not done — and two composite indicators were created to streamline overlapping elements.

Why it matters — bystander CPR quality drives survival, yet registries and researchers have been measuring it inconsistently. A shared metric would let out-of-hospital cardiac arrest data here in Australia and overseas speak the same language, and it puts system factors like dispatcher instruction on the same footing as compression depth.

The caveats — this is a provisional metric. It hasn't been validated yet, so it's context rather than practice change. And attrition was heavy — only eighteen panellists made it to the final consensus round.

Quick recap. One — a UK-led Delphi panel agreed on eleven indicators for measuring bystander CPR quality, from compression mechanics through to dispatcher instruction and AED use, though the metric still needs validating.
