Papers in this episode

  • Risk-score-guided conveyance safe in chest pain

    Heart · 3 Oct 2026 · RCT · Hypothesis-generating

    A Dutch pragmatic RCT testing whether a prehospital risk score could guide ambulance conveyance in 5170 adults with undifferentiated chest pain — it was non-inferior to usual care for 3-day serious adverse events while increasing non-conveyance, cutting 30-day ED presentations and lowering costs.

  • Stroke care gaps for First Nations patients

    The Medical Journal of Australia · 3 Oct 2026 · Cohort · Context only

    Victorian linked-data study of 32,163 EMS-attended stroke/TIA patients found First Nations patients had more than double the age-standardised incidence of EMS attendance, were less likely to receive a paramedic stroke assessment (58.2% vs 67.3%) and less often taken to thrombectomy-capable hospitals, though adjusted mortality was similar.

  • What OHCA care is worth paying for

    Prehospital Emergency Care · 3 Oct 2026 · Systematic review · Context only

    Systematic review of 10 economic evaluations of prehospital OHCA care found early defibrillation and EMT-delivered ALS, prehospital ECPR, and termination-of-resuscitation rules are all cost-effective, while health-equity reporting across the studies was poor.

  • Nearly half of paediatric doses off-protocol

    Prehospital Emergency Care · 3 Oct 2026 · Cohort · Hypothesis-generating

    US quality-registry cohort of 386 prehospital paediatric medication administrations found 46.4% deviated at least 20% from protocolised dosing — mostly underdoses — with ondansetron (aOR 36.9) and the intranasal route (aOR 7.3) the biggest drivers.

  • Prehospital vasopressors in trauma unproven

    European Journal of Emergency Medicine · 3 Oct 2026 · Systematic review · Hypothesis-generating

    Systematic review of five retrospective studies (31,849 trauma patients) found prehospital vasopressor infusions before haemorrhage control showed inconsistent mortality associations — one military study found markedly lower adjusted survival and another linked vasopressors to more massive transfusion and coagulopathy — on very-low-certainty evidence.

  • Methoxyflurane feasibility confirmed in Canada

    Pain Management · 3 Oct 2026 · Cohort · Supports current practice

    Canadian feasibility study of patient-controlled inhaled methoxyflurane in 97 adult trauma patients found 95% protocol compliance, median pain falling from 8/10 to 5/10, mostly mild side effects in 23%, and no serious drug-related adverse events.

  • Bigger crews work faster, don't save more

    Journal of Trauma and Injury · 3 Oct 2026 · Registry · Context only

    Korean nationwide cohort of 78,203 severe trauma patients found crews of three or more achieved better prehospital IV access (aOR 1.47) and scene times ≤10 minutes (aOR 1.09) than two-person crews, with no survival difference after adjustment.

  • EMS and ED disagree on paediatric asthma signs

    Prehospital Emergency Care · 3 Oct 2026 · Cross-sectional · Context only

    Prospective study of 295 children transported with asthma exacerbations found only slight-to-fair agreement between EMS and ED clinicians on asthma severity exam findings, suggesting a prehospital paediatric asthma severity score would be hard to validate.

  • Two-thirds of stroke patients delay seeking care

    Cerebrovascular Diseases · 3 Oct 2026 · Systematic review · Context only

    Meta-analysis of 27 studies and 266,336 ischaemic stroke patients across 14 countries found 67.7% experienced prehospital delay before seeking care, with stroke knowledge and symptom recognition the key modifiable drivers.

  • Direct geriatric admission depends on geography

    BMC Geriatrics · 3 Oct 2026 · Cross-sectional · Context only

    Swedish register study of 8208 ambulance-transported patients aged ≥65 in Stockholm found only 7% went directly to a geriatric unit instead of an ED, with conveyance rates varying fourfold (4–21%) between hospital service areas and largely explained by distance, ownership and staffed-bed numbers.

  • US racial gaps in trauma interventions

    JAMA Surgery · 3 Oct 2026 · Registry · Context only

    US trauma–EMS linked cohort of 18,280 injured adults found Black patients received fewer prehospital airway interventions and intubations than White patients, and patients from disadvantaged neighbourhoods received fewer prehospital interventions overall.

Transcript

Eleven papers to get through today. Paper one — a Dutch trial shows a prehospital risk score can safely guide which chest pain patients stay home. Paper two — Australian linked data shows First Nations stroke patients get less paramedic stroke assessment and less often reach thrombectomy-capable hospitals. Paper three — early defibrillation, prehospital ECPR and termination-of-resuscitation rules all pass the cost-effectiveness test. Paper four — nearly half of prehospital paediatric drug doses deviated from protocol, mostly underdoses. Paper five — prehospital vasopressors in trauma remain unproven. Paper six — methoxyflurane holds up in Canadian real-world use. Paper seven — bigger crews work faster on trauma scenes but don't save more lives. Paper eight — paramedics and emergency doctors barely agree on paediatric asthma signs. Paper nine — two-thirds of stroke patients delay calling for help. Paper ten — direct admission of older patients to geriatric units depends more on geography than need. Paper eleven — US data shows Black trauma patients receive fewer prehospital interventions.

Paper one — Heart. A Dutch pragmatic randomised trial, five thousand one hundred and seventy adults with undifferentiated chest pain, tested whether conveyance guided by the prehospital PreHEART score was safe. It was non-inferior to usual care for serious adverse events at three days — win ratio of one point zero one. Non-conveyance rose to thirty-four point six per cent against twenty-four and a half, thirty-day emergency presentations fell, and costs dropped by three hundred and forty-five euros per patient. Crucially, no deaths within three days among the fifteen hundred and twenty-six patients left at home. The caveat — it ran in the Dutch system with its own consult-and-convey structure, so the safety numbers don't transfer automatically. But it's the strongest evidence yet that risk-score-guided non-conveyance in chest pain can be safe.

Paper two — The Medical Journal of Australia, and it's Australian data. A Victorian data-linkage study of thirty-two thousand one hundred and sixty-three stroke and TIA patients attended by ambulance found First Nations patients had more than double the age-standardised rate of EMS attendance — three hundred and forty-three versus one hundred and forty-four per hundred thousand person-years. They were also less likely to receive a paramedic stroke assessment — fifty-eight per cent against sixty-seven — and less often taken to a thrombectomy-capable hospital, twenty-four versus thirty-three per cent. Adjusted mortality was similar. Only two hundred and eighty-seven First Nations patients, so precision is limited — but the stroke assessment gap is the piece that sits squarely in crews' hands.

Paper three — a systematic review of ten economic evaluations of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest care. Early defibrillation and advanced life support delivered by basic-tier crews were cost-effective, prehospital ECPR was more cost-effective than in-hospital ECPR, and termination-of-resuscitation rules saved money. The numbers come from different health systems and funding models, and equity reporting was poor — but for Australian services weighing defibrillator coverage, ECPR pathways and termination rules, this is useful ballast.

Paper four — a US quality registry, three hundred and eighty-six medication administrations in two hundred and forty children. Forty-six per cent of doses deviated at least twenty per cent from protocol, and eighty per cent of those were underdoses. Ondansetron was the worst offender — eighty-five per cent off-protocol — and the intranasal route wasn't far behind at seventy per cent. Weight-estimation error wasn't the driver, which points at fixed-dose formulations and delivery-device limits instead. Weight-based paediatric dosing and intranasal delivery are standard practice here in Australia, so those error patterns translate directly.

Paper five — a systematic review of five retrospective studies covering nearly thirty-two thousand trauma patients, on prehospital vasopressor infusions before haemorrhage control. The mortality signals were mixed — one military study found markedly lower adjusted survival with vasopressors, and another linked them to more massive transfusion and coagulopathy. Very low certainty evidence with inevitable confounding — but nothing here supports the practice. Continuous vasopressor infusions in trauma aren't something most Australian ambulances run anyway.

Paper six — a Canadian feasibility study of patient-controlled inhaled methoxyflurane in ninety-seven adult trauma patients. Ninety-five per cent protocol compliance, median pain scores fell from eight out of ten to five, side effects in just under a quarter and mostly mild, and no serious drug-related events. No comparator arm, so the pain relief can't be pinned on the drug alone. But with the green whistle already standard kit across Australian services, independent real-world safety data is welcome.

Paper seven — a Korean nationwide registry of seventy-eight thousand two hundred severe trauma patients. Crews of three or more got IV access in hypotensive patients more often and hit ten-minute scene times more often than two-person crews — but survival to discharge was identical after adjustment. Korean staffing and skill mix differ from Australian models, but the message is that a third crew member buys speed and procedures, not lives.

Paper eight — a prospective study of two hundred and ninety-five children transported with asthma, comparing paramedic and emergency department exam findings. Agreement was slight for suprasternal retractions and only fair for tachypnoea and accessory muscle use. Some of that may be timing — the EMS surveys were completed after ED arrival, when the examination may already have changed. But it suggests a prehospital paediatric asthma severity score would be hard to validate anywhere.

Paper nine — a meta-analysis of twenty-seven studies and over two hundred and sixty-six thousand ischaemic stroke patients across fourteen countries. Sixty-eight per cent delayed seeking care before hospital, sixty-five per cent took more than two hours, and seventy-seven per cent more than three. Low-certainty evidence, dominated by Asian studies — but onset-to-call delay is exactly what Australian stroke awareness campaigns target, and this says that work is far from finished.

Paper ten — a Swedish register study from Stockholm, eight thousand two hundred older patients transported by ambulance. Only seven per cent went directly to a geriatric unit instead of an ED, and conveyance rates varied fourfold between hospital service areas — explained mostly by distance, ownership and staffed bed numbers rather than patient need. Australia doesn't have a matching geriatric-unit structure, so this is context for the alternative-destinations debate, not a pathway to copy.

Paper eleven — a US registry of eighteen thousand two hundred and eighty injured adults. Black patients were about half as likely to be intubated prehospital as White patients — seven point nine versus four per cent — and patients from disadvantaged neighbourhoods received fewer prehospital interventions overall, including prehospital blood. The categories and trauma structures don't map neatly onto Australia — prehospital blood is still limited to a few Australian services — but the method is worth borrowing: audit your own service's data for these gaps.

Quick recap. One — a prehospital chest-pain risk score made non-conveyance cheaper, with no extra harm. Two — First Nations stroke patients get less paramedic assessment and miss thrombectomy-capable hospitals more often. Three — defib, prehospital ECPR and termination rules all pass the cost-effectiveness test. Four — nearly half of paediatric doses run off-protocol, mostly underdoses. Five — prehospital vasopressors in trauma remain unproven. Six — the green whistle holds up in Canadian real-world use. Seven — bigger crews work faster on trauma scenes but don't save more lives. Eight — paramedics and EDs barely agree on paediatric asthma signs. Nine — two-thirds of stroke patients delay calling for help. Ten — direct geriatric admission depends more on geography than need. Eleven — US trauma data shows big racial gaps in prehospital interventions.
